> [!NOTE]  Pitch for Mid-Term!
> 
> Fast fashion. I think we're all aware of the phenonemon. But just how fast is it?
> 
> The average American now [throws away 37 kilograms of clothing per year](https://earth.org/statistics-about-fast-fashion-waste/)
> 
Why do we fill our already overflowing closets with clothes we’ll eventually get bored of? By the time we realize we're stuck in this cycle, a ton of money has been wasted, there's no space left, and we're drowning in guilt. 
>
Yet, the easier solution is often just to make enough room for a few more clothes by getting rid of the old ones somehow, keeping the cycle going.
But why does this keep happening? 
>
For people aged 18–25 specifically, this is a phase of self-discovery, which means a lot of trial and error with clothing and a ton of social media influence. After speaking with 9 interviewees in this age group, I learned that the aftermath of this trial and error really weighs on them. They recognize that they resort to impulse buys for mood alleviation, a distraction, or just to have parcel in the mail to look forward to. 
>
I want to help by designing an intervention that takes place right at the cart. Just as you're about to check out, the goal is to spark a moment of reflection that might deter those impulse clothing buys. 
> 
> Why the cart, though? Usually, the purchase pipeline starts right when we're scrolling on social media, so how will intervening at the cart actually help? Based on contextual research with a participant and my interview responses, people naturally pause at the cart. It's where they break out of autopilot mode and think—_really_ think. I want to add an extra layer of thought and meet them where they already are.



I looked at similar interventions: 

1) The Impulse Judge
- Playful web extension with a set of "roasts" the user is forced to type in. 

1) Think Twice, Buy Once
- Simple mobile app that pushes users to wait a day or two before making a purchase.
